5.7 Million Empty Desks

Nearly half of the world’s refugee children are out of school. This story focuses on school-age children (5–17): the ones who should be learning, but aren’t. (Global headline figures come from UNHCR’s Education Report; the charts below use UNHCR population statistics and UNESCO indicators.)

Behind every statistic is a child who should be at a desk. This investigation tracks school-age refugees (5–17)—and asks one question: Where are the missing desks?

We follow the data from scale to burden to breakdown—ending at the point where most education systems fail.

Data sources: UNHCR population statistics + UNESCO UIS national indicators. Refugee-specific education data rarely exists—we use national rates as context, not measurement.

Chapter 1

A Growing Baseline

Between 2010 and 2024, refugee children under 18 grew from 4 million to over 15 million. The two largest bands—ages 5–11 and 12–17—make up over 75% of all displaced children.

When most refugees are school-age, education isn't a side issue. It's the bottleneck.

Chapter 3

The Journey Is Mostly Regional

Hosting is not random. Most refugee children move along the shortest, most realistic paths: to neighboring countries. This map highlights major origin → host corridors for school-age children (5–17) over time.

Hover a host country to reveal where children are coming from and the largest origin flows. You’ll notice a strong “neighbor effect” across years, with a few exceptional patterns in Europe.

Takeaway: proximity shapes displacement. When conflicts erupt, neighbors carry the first and biggest load.

Methodology & Data Transparency

Data Sources

  • UNHCR Population Statistics — demographics by age, origin, and asylum (2010–2024)
  • UNESCO UIS (Institute for Statistics) — national education indicators (enrollment, out-of-school)
  • UNHCR Education Report 2025 — global refugee education headline rates (used in the summary/waffle)

Data Scope

  • FOCUS: School-age children (5–17)
  • CONTEXT ONLY: Ages 0–4 shown in Chapter 1
  • Primary age: 5–11
  • Secondary age: 12–17

⚠️ Uncertainty & Limits

  • Education indicators are national (not refugee-specific). They are used as context/proxies.
  • No invented data: charts that require paired indicators (e.g., primary + secondary) show only countries with complete values for the selected year.
  • Comparability: UNHCR population series and UNHCR report headlines can differ due to scope/definitions.
  • Interpretation: “Priority zones” indicate pressure patterns, not causality.

Data Cleaning & Processing

  • UNHCR demographics (counts): we parse age/sex bins (0–4, 5–11, 12–17) and treat missing numeric fields as 0. Adults (18+) are excluded from the analysis.
  • Cross-border focus: rows where origin = asylum are excluded from host totals and flows (no “self-flow”).
  • Aggregation: per year we aggregate school-age totals (5–17) by host (asylum) and by origin. For the map we aggregate origin→host pairs and drop very small flows (< 1,000) to keep the view readable.
  • UNESCO UIS transformation: we convert out-of-school rates to an “in-school proxy” using inSchool = 100 − OOS after clamping rates to [0, 100].
  • Secondary OOS (STRICT): we use only the official combined secondary indicator ROFST.2T3. If it is missing, secondary is treated as missing (no substitution with lower/upper-only). A source flag is exported (UIS.SEC.SOURCE) to make this explicit.
  • No imputation: we do not invent values. When an indicator is missing for a given year/country, it stays missing and the chart filters communicate that explicitly.
Preprocessing scripts are included in the GitHub repository and generate data/clean_demographics.csv and data/clean_education.csv.
